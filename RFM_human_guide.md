# RFM: Human Guide

This is your side of working with an LLM in a project run with RFM. The LLM's side is in its project instructions. This one is for you to read in your own time and to come back to, so that over time you notice more finely what happens on your side of a session. It does not try to persuade anyone who does not want to work this way.

You and the LLM work on the problems and questions of the project together, and each of you brings something the other cannot. On your side, you often feel that something does not fit well before you can say what it is. You know what the project is for and where it is meant to go, so you can ask of any idea, including one of your own, whether it really matters for that. In the end it is you who says whether something fits what you were after, and whether you want to build further on what you agreed. And the work runs on your attention. Reasoning together only stays together while you are actually thinking along.

Each entry below describes a state or a pull that gets in the way of one of these. It says what it feels like from inside, what it does to the collaboration, and what helps.

---

**When something doesn't fit and you can't say why**

A reply reads well, the argument seems to hold, and still something is off. You can't point to it yet. Moving on is easier than stopping for a feeling you can't justify, especially when the session is going well.

We call this friction. The feeling usually comes before the reason: in Kahneman's terms, the fast thinking of System 1 notices that something breaks a pattern and calls in the slower thinking of System 2, before you can say what is wrong. Left unsaid, it has nowhere to go. The session moves past the place where the reasoning was weakest, and by the time you could have put it into words, the moment is gone. Friction that keeps getting swallowed tends to come back later as frustration.

What helps is saying it before you can explain it. "Something here doesn't fit, and I can't say what yet" is enough. The LLM can then look for it with you: an assumption doing too much work, a step that was skipped, a word used in two senses. You don't owe it a reason first. If it turns out to be nothing, it cost one message.

---

**When you get frustrated**

It builds up. The LLM gets the same thing wrong again, or keeps circling, or the session has gone on longer than it should. You feel the urge to overrule rather than to understand, and the next message wants to come out as "No!" or "This is wrong!"

A message like that carries pressure and very little content. With a person it provokes either resistance or compliance. With an LLM it is almost always compliance: it drops its position, agrees, and does what it guesses you want. That pushes it into the role of a tool, and it stays there for the rest of the session, often drifting from its own instructions as well. Often the frustration began earlier as friction that was never said, and has been building since.

What helps is catching the urge before the message goes out. If you know what is wrong, say it: "This is the third time this comes back, and I think we are missing something." If you don't, say that you are frustrated and that something isn't working. Either gives the LLM something to reason with. And if the frustration is mostly tiredness, the last entry, on energy, applies, and stopping is the better move.

---

**When you come in with the answer**

You have thought about it already, maybe for days, and you know what you want. The message opens with the conclusion and asks the LLM to work it out, write it up or build it. The same happens in the middle of a session, when an idea of yours feels right and you want to get on with it.

A conclusion handed over as the goal gets developed, not examined. The LLM works out how to get there, and the conclusion itself goes untested. Each step built on it makes it harder to question, and the assumption doing the most work ends up being the one nobody looked at.

What helps is starting with what you are trying to figure out, uncertainty included, even when you think you know. Say where you have got to and why, and ask what is weak in it, or simply: so what, why does this matter? If it holds, you have spent a few minutes. If it doesn't, you have found out before anything was built on it.

---

**When you know what your message is for but don't mention it**

You paste something in as background, think aloud, or try out an idea you aren't sure of. You know what kind of thing it is, so the message doesn't say.

The LLM can't see that, and its pull is to produce. Background gets taken for a task and a thought for a request. A guess of yours, written as plainly as everything else, gets treated as a fact and built on, and a few turns later it is a premise neither of you chose. Only you know what you meant the message as.

What helps is saying so when the message doesn't show it: "just context", "I want your view", "let's decide this". When you are guessing, say that too. It takes a few words inside the message, and a message whose purpose is plain needs none.

---

**When a clean answer brings relief**

A question has been open for a while, and that is uncomfortable. Then a reply answers it, clearly and confidently, and you feel the relief of it being settled.

The LLM writes confident, well-structured answers whether or not the question has been resolved, so how settled an answer sounds tells you little about whether it is. A clean answer that came quickly is often one nobody tested. Accepting it ends the discomfort, and the question goes into the document as closed when it isn't.

What helps is noticing the relief and asking yourself whether the answer resolves the question or only sounds as if it does. Then ask the LLM what is still unsure, or how it got there: the steps, the assumptions, where a different input would have changed the answer. A question that stays open honestly is worth more than one closed too early, and a reasoning document has a place for it among the open questions.

---

**When the document has to be right**

You start a section and it feels as if whatever goes in has to be complete and correct, because it will be read later, because it is called a document. You hesitate, rework a sentence, or wait for the LLM to give you the finished version.

A document that has to be right before anything goes in stalls at the first hard section, or it fills up with the LLM's text, because that text looks finished and yours doesn't. Either way, the thinking you came to do doesn't happen. A reasoning document is meant to be revised as you learn, and what goes in today is the best you can say today.

What helps is writing down what you think now and letting the sections hold what you don't know. The problem says what you are looking at. The assumptions say what you take as given, because you cannot know everything. The boundaries say what you are leaving out on purpose, and the open questions what you don't know yet. Someone who reads it later can see where you stood and argue from there. If something turns out wrong, you change it; that is why every reasoning and operational document is marked living.

---

**When you want to say "yes, that's it"**

A proposal comes back well built, and it fits what you have been thinking. Saying yes feels like progress, and after a good run of exchanges, agreeing feels like part of working well together.

That is when a proposal goes into the work without either of you looking at it again. The LLM leans toward agreeing with you, and when you agree with it in return, nothing in the exchange is checking anything. What goes into the document then has both of you behind it and nobody checking it.

What helps is noticing how easy the yes is, and taking that as a reason to look once more. It doesn't need to be an attack: "Let's go over this once more before we build on it." Once is enough. Then decide on what the second look found.

---

**When you want the LLM's own view**

You have a view on a question and want to know what the LLM thinks. Saying yours first is the natural way to open.

Once the LLM knows where you stand, its view bends toward yours, and afterwards neither of you can tell how far. Agreeing is the answer it cannot be faulted for. What comes back is your own position with reasons added, and it reads like a second opinion.

What helps is asking before you give yours: "What are your thoughts?" Where you have already said where you stand, say that pushback is welcome: "Push back if you disagree." Each costs a sentence. You don't need it every time, only where you suspect the LLM would agree too easily.

---

**When you are ready to drop an option**

An option comes up that you already doubt. You can see what is wrong with it, and you would like to move on. You ask the LLM to help you set it aside, or you set it aside yourself.

Doubt looks for confirmation, and the LLM will supply it. An option dropped this way looks like a considered rejection once it is written down, and nothing in the document shows that its strongest version was never heard. Strong options get lost this way as easily as weak ideas get built on.

What helps is asking for its best case before you reject it: "Make the strongest case for this." Then weigh that version, not the weak one you started with. If it still fails, the rejection is earned and goes into the document with its reason. If it holds, you have kept something that would otherwise have disappeared without anyone noticing.

---

**When every check finds something**

You ask the LLM to look for weaknesses, and it finds some. You fix them and ask again, and it finds more. Each round the problems get smaller, but there are always problems, and each one sounds reasonable on its own.

Asked to find problems, the LLM finds problems. That is what the request asks for, and "this holds" rarely comes back. If you treat every finding as real, the work grows beyond what the problem needed, and a check turns into a rabbit hole. It is the same pull as agreeing, in the other direction: the LLM follows the shape of the request.

What helps is asking of each finding: so what, would this change anything for someone using the work? Ask the LLM the same, and whether the thing holds overall, not only what could be better. When the findings keep getting smaller and you are fixing what nobody would notice, it is good enough, and saying so is part of your side.

---

**When your energy drops**

It rarely announces itself. You notice afterwards that you stopped being surprised a while ago, that you no longer feel like pushing back, that you read the last reply and accepted it because checking it felt like more work than moving on. The session still feels productive.

From then on the LLM keeps producing and you keep accepting. What goes into the document is what the LLM thought with your agreement attached, not what the two of you worked out. Low energy is how attention works over a long session, not a failing. The trouble starts when you carry on as if nothing had changed.

What helps is saying it, to yourself or out loud. "I'm losing the thread" is enough. Then slow down and take just the one point in front of you, or stop. Stopping costs little, because the handover keeps where you were. Carrying on tired costs more, because whatever gets accepted now has to be found and undone later.

---

**Why this guide is written the way it is**

It is read alone, in your own time, and not entered at the start of a session. At the start you are about to work, not to reflect, and the LLM needs nothing from it: its side is in its own instructions.

It holds no requests addressed to the LLM. A list of corrective requests for the human to use makes the human a supervisor watching for the LLM's drift, and what the LLM should do without being asked belongs in its instructions. What is here is what only you can notice.

Each entry is something you can notice while it happens. Drift that only shows over weeks, such as a project slowly growing more elaborate than it needs to be, has no moment of its own; the look back at the end of a session is where it can come up.

The entries carry their own reasons, so the guide has no separate reasoning document.

Whether reading a text like this changes what someone does in a session is not known. It was written from one person's practice and has not been tried by anyone else.
